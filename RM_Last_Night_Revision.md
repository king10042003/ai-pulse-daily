# SECTION A: CORE CONCEPTS

## MODULE 1: Introduction to Research

### 🔥 What is Research?
Research is a systematic, scientific investigation to identify solutions to problems — creating new knowledge or applying existing knowledge in innovative ways. It typically starts with a question or problem and moves through investigation, analysis, and hypothesis testing.

### 🔥 Objectives of Research
- **General objective** — the broad overarching goal of a study.
- **Specific objectives** — the general objective broken into smaller, logically connected goals (defines who, what, why, when, how).

### 🔥 Characteristics of Good Research
- Focuses on the most pressing/relevant issues
- Systematic and methodical (follows a structured process)
- Objective and unbiased — conclusions must be backed by data/facts
- Replicable and verifiable
- Based on empirical evidence, not just reasoning

### 🔥 Research Methods vs Research Methodology (frequently asked)
| Research Methods | Research Methodology |
|---|---|
| The actual tools/techniques/procedures used to collect data and solve the problem (surveys, experiments, statistical tools) | The overall systematic, scientific **approach/philosophy** of how research should be conducted |
| Answers "what methods do I use" | Answers "why/how do I choose this method" |
| Practical, applied | Conceptual, theoretical framework guiding method choice |

### 🔥 Research Approaches
- **Deductive**: theory → hypothesis → test with data → confirm/reject → modify theory if needed. (Top-down: general to specific.)
- **Inductive**: observations → look for patterns → build theory/explanation. (Bottom-up: specific to general.) Researcher can change direction as new patterns emerge.
- **Abductive**: starts with a "surprising fact" existing theories can't explain; searches for the *best available explanation*, often combining inductive + deductive reasoning.
- **Quantitative**: numerical data, statistical analysis, large samples, structured methods.
- **Qualitative**: non-statistical, in-depth understanding of a phenomenon in natural settings, small samples (~6–10 people), open-ended questions. Methods: one-to-one interviews, focus groups, ethnographic research, content analysis.

### Research Proposal — Key Elements
A proposal must answer: **what** we want to do, **why**, and **how**. Includes: research problem/goal, objectives (general + specific), literature review, hypothesis, variables (independent/dependent), scope/limitations, sampling plan, data collection tools, data analysis plan, ethical approval.

**Common proposal errors:** overly broad/vague goals, missing literature review, insufficient methodology detail, unrealistic timeline, poor budget justification.

### 🔥 Literature Review
Purpose: gives background knowledge, helps formulate hypotheses, suggests appropriate data sources/methodology/statistical techniques, helps interpret findings by comparison with existing studies.
**Sources:** books/indices, periodicals/journals, encyclopedias, government publications, research institution publications, newspapers.

### 🔥 Errors in Research (common exam topic)
- **Population Specification Error** — surveying the wrong target population.
- **Sampling Frame Error** — using an incorrect/incomplete list to draw the sample from.
- **Selection Error** — bias in who ends up responding (e.g., only choosing convenient/similar respondents).
- **Non-response Error** — gap between intended sample and actual respondents; two types: **non-contact** (couldn't reach respondent) and **refusal** (respondent declined).
- **Surrogate Information Error** — data collected doesn't actually match what's needed to answer the research question.
- **Experimental Error** — mismatch between the "true effect" and the "effect attributed to" the independent variable (e.g., confusing correlation with causation).

## Types of Research Design

### 🔥 Pure (Basic) vs Applied Research
- **Pure/Basic/Fundamental Research** — seeks to expand fundamental knowledge/theory, not solve an immediate practical problem.
- **Applied Research** — uses findings from basic research to solve specific, real-world problems (e.g., improving hiring processes, workplace efficiency).

### 🔥 Causal vs Correlational
- **Causal** — establishes cause-and-effect between variables (often via experiments, manipulating one variable).
- **Correlational** — examines the relationship/association between two variables **without manipulating** either one; only tells you if/how they move together, not causation.

### 🔥 Cross-sectional vs Longitudinal
- **Cross-sectional** — data collected at a **single point in time**, comparing different groups (like a snapshot/photograph).
- **Longitudinal** — data collected **over an extended period**, tracking the same subjects; better at suggesting cause-effect relationships, but takes much longer.

### 🔥 Experimental, Semi-experimental (Quasi), Non-Experimental
- **Experimental** — researcher manipulates one or more control/independent variables and measures the effect; used to establish cause-effect.
- **Quasi/Semi-experimental** — similar to experimental but **without full random assignment** to groups (used when true randomization isn't practical/ethical).
- **Non-experimental** — no manipulation of variables at all; purely observational.

### Descriptive vs Exploratory
- **Descriptive** — describes characteristics of a population/phenomenon as it is (what, where, when).
- **Exploratory** — used when a problem isn't well understood yet; aims to gain insight and generate hypotheses for future research, rather than testing them.

## Research Modeling
- **Types of models**: descriptive, predictive, normative (prescriptive), simulation-based.
- **Model building stages**: problem definition → identify variables → formulate the model → validate/test → refine.
- **Heuristic modeling**: uses rule-of-thumb/experience-based approaches to find a "good enough" solution when an exact method is too complex.
- **Simulation modeling**: mimics a real system's behavior over time using a computer model, often used when direct experimentation is impractical/costly.

## MODULE 2: Sampling

### 🔥 What is Sampling & Why We Sample
Sampling = selecting a representative subset from a larger population to draw conclusions about the whole population, without having to study everyone.

**Why sample instead of studying the whole population:**
- Large populations make full study impractical/costly
- Some populations are hard to access (e.g., prisoners, disaster survivors)
- Destructive testing (e.g., testing a fuse destroys it — can't test all of them)
- A well-chosen sample can sometimes be **more accurate** than a poorly executed full census

### 🔥 Steps in the Sampling Process
1. Define the population
2. Choose the sampling frame (list/database of population members)
3. Select the sampling method (probability or non-probability)
4. Determine sample size
5. Collect the sample
6. Analyze the sample

### 🔥 Probability Sampling Techniques (each member has a known, non-zero chance of selection)
- **Simple Random Sampling** — every member has equal chance of selection (like drawing names from a hat).
  - Formula: `Probability of selection = Sample size / Population size`
- **Systematic Sampling** — select every k-th member from a list.
  - Formula: `Skip interval (k) = Population size (N) / Sample size (n)`
  - Example: N=591, n=25 → k = 591/25 = 23.64 ≈ 23. Pick a random start between 1–23, then every 23rd person after.
- **Stratified Sampling** — divide population into subgroups (strata) based on shared characteristics, then randomly sample from each stratum. Ensures representation across subgroups but risks bias in how strata are defined.
- **Cluster Sampling** — randomly select entire groups/clusters (e.g., geographic areas) rather than individuals; can be single-stage (use whole cluster) or two-stage (randomly sample within the selected cluster too).
- **Multistage Sampling** — combines multiple sampling methods across stages.

### 🔥 Non-Probability Sampling Techniques (selection is not random; convenience-driven)
- **Convenience Sampling** — choosing whoever is easiest to access.
- **Snowball Sampling** — existing participants recruit future participants from among their acquaintances (useful for hard-to-reach populations, but introduces network bias).
- **Judgmental/Purposive Sampling** — researcher deliberately selects based on judgment of who's most useful/relevant.
- **Quota Sampling** — non-random selection until a fixed quota per subgroup is filled.

### Sampling Errors & Challenges
- **Sampling Bias** — certain population members systematically over/under-represented.
- **Non-response** — selected participants don't respond.
- **Sampling Error** — natural difference between sample results and true population values, because a sample can never perfectly represent the population.

## Data Collection

### 🔥 Primary vs Secondary Data
| Primary Data | Secondary Data |
|---|---|
| First-hand, original data collected specifically for the current research | Data already collected/published by someone else for another purpose |
| More accurate, specific, but time/cost-intensive | Cheaper, faster, but may not perfectly fit the current research need |
| Collected via: surveys, experiments, observation, interviews | Found in: books, journals, government publications, databases |

### Methods of Primary Data Collection
- **Observation Method** — systematically watching and recording behavior (common in behavioral science).
- **Interview Method**: Personal (face-to-face), Structured (fixed questions) vs Unstructured (flexible), Focused, Clinical, Non-directive.
- **Questionnaires** — self-administered; benefits: no interviewer bias, respondents can answer at their own pace; drawbacks: low response rates, can't clarify unclear answers, no follow-up once sent.
- **Schedules** — similar to questionnaires but filled out by trained enumerators, not the respondent directly.

### 🔥 Questionnaire Design — Steps
1. Identify the scope/purpose of research
2. Keep language simple, avoid jargon
3. Decide question types (open vs closed-ended) based on data needed
4. Know your audience
5. Choose a good survey tool/platform
6. Save sensitive/personal questions for last (improves completion rates)

### Types of Questions
- **Open-ended** — free-text response, rich qualitative data.
- **Closed-ended** — fixed choices, easier to analyze quantitatively (e.g., multiple choice, Likert scale).

### 🔥 Data Validity Types (commonly tested)
- **External Validity** — how well findings generalize to other settings/populations/times.
- **Criterion Validity** — how well a measure predicts an outcome based on another established measure (concurrent = correlates with an existing validated measure; predictive = predicts future performance).
- **Content Validity** — whether a test covers all relevant aspects of the construct being measured.
- **Face Validity** — a subjective/superficial judgment of whether a test "looks like" it measures what it claims to (weakest form of validity).

### Data Editing & Coding
- **Data Reliability** — checking source, method, precision, potential bias.
- **Data Adequacy** — checking data isn't too narrow/broad in scope for current research.
- **Data Validation** — verifying data values come from an acceptable/expected range/set (a form of data cleansing).

## MODULE 3: Data Collection and Experiments

### 🔥 Design of Experiments — Types
- **Pre-Experimental Design** — weakest design, no control group or randomization (e.g., one-group pretest-posttest).
- **Quasi-Experimental Design** — has manipulation of independent variable but **lacks full random assignment** to groups.
- **True Experimental Design** — has both manipulation of independent variable **and** random assignment to control/treatment groups — the gold standard for establishing causation.
- **Factorial Experimental Design** — studies the effect of **two or more independent variables simultaneously**, including their interaction effects.

### 🔥 Basic Principles of Experimental Design
- **Replication** — repeating the experiment/treatment on multiple experimental units to increase reliability and estimate error.
- **Randomization** — randomly assigning treatments to experimental units to eliminate systematic bias.
- **Blocking** — grouping similar experimental units together ("blocks") to control for a known source of variability, isolating the effect of the treatment more precisely.
- **Validity** — whether the experiment actually measures what it intends to (internal validity = confidence effect is due to treatment, not confounds; external validity = generalizability).
- **Reliability** — consistency of results if the study were repeated.

### Data Collection Methods (Module 3 recap)
- **Observation & Interview** — direct methods of gathering data from subjects.
- **Case Studies** — in-depth study of a single subject/instance/organization.
- **Questionnaires** — see Module 2 above; guidelines include simplicity, logical question order, avoiding jargon, adequate response space.

### Handling Data Issues
- **"Don't Know" Responses** — occur when respondent lacks knowledge/opinion; must be handled carefully in coding (shouldn't be forced into a substantive category).
- **Missing Data** — data not recorded for some respondents/variables; needs careful handling (exclusion, imputation) to avoid bias.
- **Data Editing** — reviewing collected data for completeness, consistency, and accuracy before analysis.
- **Data Coding Rules** — assigning numerical/categorical codes to responses systematically to enable statistical analysis.

## MODULE 4: Models and Hypothesis Testing

### 🔥 What is a Hypothesis?
A hypothesis is a statement/claim about a research problem or population parameter that can be tested using sample data. It may be logical, empirical/working, or statistical.

### 🔥 Null Hypothesis (H0) vs Alternative Hypothesis (H1)
- **H0 (Null Hypothesis)** — states there is **no** relationship/effect/difference; the default assumption we test against.
- **H1 (Alternative Hypothesis)** — the statement we accept if we reject H0; represents the effect/relationship we're actually investigating.

### 🔥 Type I and Type II Errors (VERY commonly asked)
| Error | Description |
|---|---|
| **Type I Error (α)** | Rejecting H0 when it's actually **true** (false positive) |
| **Type II Error (β)** | Failing to reject (accepting) H0 when it's actually **false** (false negative) |

**Memory trick:** Type I = "crying wolf" when there's no wolf. Type II = missing the wolf that's actually there.

### 🔥 One-Tailed vs Two-Tailed Tests
- **One-tailed test**: H1 states the parameter is either greater than OR less than (not both) — `H1: μ < μ0` or `H1: μ > μ0`. Used when direction of effect matters/is predicted in advance.
- **Two-tailed test**: H1 allows the parameter to differ in **either** direction — `H1: μ ≠ μ0`. Used when you just want to detect *any* difference, direction unknown.

### 🔥 Significance Level (α) and P-value
- **Significance level (α)** — the probability threshold for rejecting H0 when it's true (commonly 0.05, 0.01, or 0.10). If α = 0.05, there's a 5% risk of a Type I error.
- **P-value** — probability of observing results as extreme as (or more extreme than) what was found, assuming H0 is true.
- **Decision rule**: If p-value < α → reject H0 (result is "statistically significant"). If p-value ≥ α → fail to reject H0.
- **Confidence level = 1 − α** (e.g., α=0.05 → 95% confidence level)

### 🔥 Rejection Region / Critical Region
- The **critical/rejection region** is the range of test statistic values that leads to rejecting H0.
- **Critical value** — the threshold separating the rejection region from the non-rejection region.
- **Left-tailed test**: H1 uses "<" — rejection region on the left.
- **Right-tailed test**: H1 uses ">" — rejection region on the right.
- **Two-tailed test**: rejection regions on both ends.

### 🔥 Hypothesis Testing Procedure (Steps — good long-answer structure)
1. State the null (H0) and alternative (H1) hypotheses.
2. Choose the significance level (α).
3. Select the appropriate test (Z-test, t-test, F-test, chi-square, etc.) based on data type/sample size.
4. Calculate the test statistic from sample data.
5. Determine the critical value / p-value.
6. Compare and decide: reject or fail to reject H0.
7. Draw a conclusion in context of the research question.

### 🔥 Degrees of Freedom
`Degrees of Freedom = N − 1` (for a single sample), or more generally `(number of observations) − (number of parameters estimated)`. Used in t-tests, chi-square tests, F-tests to determine the correct reference distribution.

## Parametric Tests

### 🔥 Z-test
- Used when population standard deviation is **known** and/or sample size is **large (n > 30)**.
- Compares a sample mean to a known population mean, or compares means of two large independent samples.
- Example: comparing average salaries of men vs women in an organization with a large sample.

### 🔥 T-test
- Used when population standard deviation is **unknown** and sample size is **small (n < 30)**.
- Assumes population is normally distributed, samples are random/independent.
- **Types**: One-sample t-test (compare sample mean to known value), Independent two-sample t-test (compare two independent groups), Paired t-test (compare before/after or matched pairs).

### 🔥 F-test
- Compares the **variances** of two independent samples (are they equally variable, or is one more spread out?).
- Core to **ANOVA** (comparing means of 3+ groups simultaneously).
- Example: comparing variability in bolt diameters from two machines.

### Z-test vs T-test — quick comparison (common short-answer/MCQ)
| | Z-test | T-test |
|---|---|---|
| Sample size | Large (n > 30) | Small (n < 30) |
| Population SD | Known | Unknown |
| Distribution used | Normal (Z) | Student's t-distribution |

### 🔥 ANOVA (Analysis of Variance)
Used to compare means of **three or more groups** simultaneously (instead of doing multiple pairwise t-tests, which would inflate error rate).

**Key components:**
- **SST (Total Sum of Squares)** = SSTR + SSE
- **SSTR (Treatment Sum of Squares)** — variation *between* group means and the grand mean.
- **SSE (Error Sum of Squares)** — variation *within* groups (random error).
- Compute **F-statistic** = (SSTR/df_treatment) / (SSE/df_error); compare to critical F-value.
- If calculated F > critical F → reject H0 (means are NOT all equal — at least one group differs).

**Single-Factor (One-Way) ANOVA**: tests the effect of **one** independent variable (factor) on a dependent variable, using designs like Completely Randomized Design (CRD).

**Fixed Effect vs Random Effect Model**: Fixed effect assumes the specific treatment levels studied are the only ones of interest; random effect treats the levels as a random sample from a larger population of possible levels.

### Bayesian Statistics (brief concept)
- Uses **prior probability** (belief before evidence) + **likelihood** (probability of evidence given hypothesis) → **posterior probability** (updated belief after evidence), via **Bayes' Rule**.
- `Posterior ∝ Prior × Likelihood`
- Useful for updating beliefs as new data arrives, rather than treating each test in isolation.

## MODULE 5: Report Writing

### 🔥 Meaning & Structure of a Research Report
A report is a formal document with three defining features: **predefined structure**, **independent sections**, and **unbiased conclusions**. Treated as a legal/official document in many workplaces.

### 🔥 Components of a Scientific Report (very commonly asked as a list-based long answer)
1. **Title Page** — subject, author/organization name
2. **Table of Contents** — chapter names + page numbers
3. **Executive Summary** — condensed overview (for lengthy reports)
4. **Introduction** — problem statement, research purpose, background
5. **Methodology/Approach** — how data was collected (primary/secondary, sample size, sampling plan, tools used)
6. **Body / Analysis and presentation of results** — data tables, quantitative analysis, findings
7. **Limitations/Constraints** — time, geography, technique, accuracy limitations
8. **Conclusions and Recommendations**
9. **Bibliography/References**
10. **Appendices** — surveys, statistical tables, technical details

### 🔥 Types of Reports
- **By delivery mode**: Oral report vs Written report.
  - Oral reports are harder to prepare — require direct audience interaction, careful planning of "what to say, how to say it, how much to say."
- **By time interval**: Daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, yearly reports.
- **By length/formality**: Short report (concise, narrow scope, e.g., monthly sales report) vs Long report (comprehensive; can be technical or non-technical, formal or informal, government report).

### Steps in Report Writing
Define purpose → collect and organize data → outline structure → write draft → review/edit → finalize with proper formatting, citations, and visual aids.

### Format and Writing Style
- Keep language simple — assume the reader is short on time and may not know research jargon.
- **Justify** every statement with facts/data — no unsupported claims.
- **Quantify** wherever data allows (avoid vague language).
- Consistent heading/subheading formatting (font sizes for chapter/section/subsection headings).
- Tables and figures: sequential numbering (either continuous like 1,2,3... or chapter-wise like Table 1.1, 1.2...) — must stay consistent throughout.

### 🔥 Citing and Referencing Sources
- **Footnotes** — numerical references within text linking to a references list.
- **Op. cit.** ("in the work cited") — referring back to a previously cited work (different page).
- **Loc. cit.** ("in the place cited") — referring back to the exact same page previously cited.
- **Ibid.** ("the same") — referring to the immediately preceding citation.
- Bibliography/References section lists only sources actually cited in the report.

### Oral Presentation Guidelines
- Clear, non-technical, strategic introduction.
- One idea per slide.
- Strong conclusion slide placed last (don't add slides after it — weakens impact).
- Use "talk outline" slides for longer presentations to orient the audience.
- Prepare for a Q&A session at the end.
